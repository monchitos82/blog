## How we almost fixed our integration environment (2026-11-15)

This post is not me spilling the beans on a secret or anything, it is more a reflection on how important it is to know your data and its workflows, even beyond other parts of your machinery. It is no wonder that some very smart people consider companies to be built around their data.

Back in 2019, when I joined my last job, I had never seen a company working at such scale, not to mention the vast diversity of processes and technologies. And everything was built by them. When you are faced with such diversity, you are quickly overwhelmed by trying to understand every piece, but in all fairness, that is way too much to achieve in just a few months. I did what every (fool) person would do and focused on the parts I was responsible for, but I missed a good chance to understand how things were architected at a higher level. I eventually learned that, and reinforced the knowledge through incidents, but probably my chance was already missed.

Anyway, from the things I learned since I joined, it was that we had basically 2 environments, but one was utterly useless to test anything else than a service running. The reason why could boil down to how hard it was to maintain the infrastructure we had, but a second reason was that it was almost impossible to produce a significant amount of traffic that would replicate that of our production environment. Well, that was the initial understanding.

As time passed, I got involved in a very exciting project focused on dark traffic patterns. Dark traffic patterns are nothing but services that are not part of the "real" release of a service. In a nutshell, these dark services were part of the same cluster but never got live traffic. Instead, these instances got forwarded traffic from the "real" instances. There was an important caveat here. Dark services (or instances in this case), could not alter the response or the data that was propagated to the downstreams of a service. Well, this was really hard. The reason why is because normally, in not vast operations, the data flows in a single way, for us we had a tiered layer of data propagation where at some point, everything was flowing through kafka. The challenge in this situation is making the production of data sufficiently isolated that no business processes were affected by the emission of testing data, which basically is what dark instances were working with.

Our intuition would dictate that if we just change configurations and produce to different topics things would be good, and while that intuition is correct, as complexity grows, it becomes really challenging to track down data's lineage and basically you end up with a black box where if not for traceability, you have zero idea what is going on within the data layer.

So we managed to isolate some services and through some incidents and initiatives, we mapped a lot of our data flows so the mentioned isolation happened. But we never managed to find a way to make our integration testing environment good enough, because we never had enough data feeding its workflows. Well, unknowingly, we had the means to feed our environment as our isolation became more and more solid, but by then, we had already shifted our focus to other needs and while we had the means to produce enough data to improve our testing environment, in reality we were missing things like data obfuscation and a way to bridge our dark instances to be able to proxy to both environments without creating a huge safety concern.

Anyway, dark traffic patterns are still used and are a great way to A/B test even before trying a canary deployment or to validate theories without impacting downstreams, but at least for me, this is just one of those _what if_ moments, where good understanding and skills could have made something exciting and new.

## Rule of law (2026-11-09)

One of my favorite courses during my master's certainly was international law. One of the principles we extensively discussed during part of it was the concept of rule of law. If you search online for it, the definition is extensive and hard to grasp, but I think our professor then did a splendid job making it simple enough to fit in a sentence, by using a legal maxim: "Everything which is not forbidden, is allowed".

That sentence stuck with me and has been useful in navigating a bunch of situations and understanding the thinking of people smarter than me, who can find loopholes and apply them towards their benefit. Is this of questionable ethics? Maybe, but again, leaving loose ends in a system is the danger you create.

And that's the itch I have as I work with agents nowadays. I think many engineers are afraid of extending capabilities to their LLMs, not because they are inherently dangerous, but because they have access to a vast amount of knowledge and they are capable of exploiting our own ignorance. This gap cannot really be solved in a single step. The agents are not inventive, but they will apply what we have taught them in spaces we haven't thought of, so understanding that not forbidding things is implicitly allowing them turns a paramount part of our design. Clarity is the name of the game, and "assuming that others will assume" is a perilous gamble.

I think agentic development is forcing us towards a broader way of thinking about solving problems. It does not stop in the dilemma of “could vs. should”; it pushes us to think not only about what good design is, but what makes a good rule good: explicit, scoped, actionable, enforceable, consistent and relevant just to mention some qualities. In the end, understanding what makes actions within our own social systems controllable is key for us to design proper agentic environments where we can get the most value out of the actions we direct.

Or, we can spend a vast amount of time reading and watching courses of people who claim they discovered the formula to master their agents just to realize they are pretty much narrating the zen of Python rebranded.

## Industrialization of code (2026-11-01)

I have discussed AI topics a couple of times in this blog, but I think there is one topic that I have not covered yet and I might be following the wrong channels, because I don’t see it being discussed in many places either. AI is changing the software industry as intelligence races towards becoming a commodity. Not only this switches several paradigms, it will push ourselves to think of software less of a pair of custom-made leather shoes and start seeing it as some sneakers instead.

I have been learning and working on understanding a bunch of principles and I see there is a lot of focus towards picking what is the right way to plan for asking AI to produce code, the more now that code can be built with a certain degree of confidence. What I don’t see everywhere is the same amount of focus towards using what the transformation into a mass-production level of industry requires. I think we are still too obsessed with laying out our tools that we don’t fully see the need for other parts of this “new” machine: measuring the outputs, measuring the processes, improving the supply chain, just to name a few. And this, personally, is quite an exciting shift, because we are speeding towards the transformation from being the artisans known as software engineers, to the industrial engineers, creating product lines and distribution channels.

I don’t have much to propose in this brief thesis. My idea takes a few analogies like the footwear one. I think we will sooner or later introduce what industrial engineering has been using for decades: quality control, workflow improvement, supply chain optimization. In all fairness, the software industry _as-is_, is not agnostic to these techniques and processes. However, the scale is changing fast and we are not only choosing between the corner shop and the multi-acre assembly line, as every individual become their own software factory, we are now forcing ourselves not only to figure out our production standards, but we are now forcing ourselves to focus on measuring the output rather than the product and between creating an experience like car dealerships or the customized Jordan sneakers checkout page, I hope we opt for the latter.

 Anyway, I am excited for this change and the opportunity it will create. We will see a new level of focus in all parts of a production process, from picking the right tools and setting them up in the right order as once Toyota engineers taught us. Improving the production lines continuously, not only the product we build. Improving the supply chains and logistics associated. Changing things like quality assurance to not only do a coverage analysis of unit cases but actually measure the output of our product lines and the quality of what is produced by our machines (agents). We will take observability to a different level. Measuring resource utilization, throughput, acceptance, rework and why not, even repurposing.

Is this enthusiasm too sudden and too naive? I don’t think so. I think many parts of this industry (the software industry) will remain, just as you get to commission a knife to a Japanese blacksmith or a pair of shoes to an Italian artisan. Demand will remain for certain classes of products, and it will require a high degree of specialized skills to still exist and improve. For the rest, I hope we improve the parts where supply is still very rough (e.g. package managers and app stores) and accept that quality is good enough for the bulk of simple and repeatable operations.
